What you will be able to doApa yang akan dapat Anda lakukan

  1. Describe what child-centred and developmentally appropriate practice looks like in a classroom, starting from children’s interests, strengths and needs.
  2. Notice a child’s capability and what may get in the way of taking part, before looking at labels.
  3. Adjust a planned experience so every child can take part and show understanding, changing the pathway but keeping the learning goal.
  4. Weigh where technology, including unplugged activities, can personalise learning and where it gets in the way.
  5. Decide what to watch for, to tell whether a support is helping or hiding a child’s learning.

What does child-centred mean?Apa arti berpusat pada anak?

Build understanding

Three ideas guide this module:

Child-centred and developmentally appropriate

Practice that starts from the children and suits where they are in their development.

Interests, needs and development

Knowing what each child is interested in, needs, and how they are developing.

Meaningful, engaging experiences

Experiences that matter to the children and hold their attention.

Child-centred does not mean educator-passive. The educator chooses, listens, notices and adjusts.
Build understanding

A window on another system. Australia’s National Quality Standard (Element 1.1.2, Child-centred) says each child’s current knowledge, strengths, ideas, culture, abilities and interests are the foundation of the program. It lists what an observer might see:

  • Educators watching, listening and talking with children for sustained periods, and paying close attention to what they say, think and do.
  • Flexibility to bring in children’s ideas, culture and interests so experiences stay relevant.
  • Children starting and revisiting their own projects, and staying deeply involved.
  • Long periods of uninterrupted time for play.
  • Educators who are alert to the many languages some children speak, and to the strategies children with additional needs use in their day.

This is a comparison, not a standard for your setting (ACECQA, n.d.).

Meet three childrenBerkenalan dengan tiga anak

These are fictional children, made up for this course. Imagine them in your school class.

Dewa (5 years)

Dewa learns by moving and building. He leads block play, balances carefully, and keeps trying when a tower falls. He finds sitting still for a long story or whole-group talk very hard, and often wanders away.

Lina (4 and a half)

At home Lina speaks a language other than Bahasa Indonesia. She understands much more than she says at school, and is quiet in group talk. She draws detailed pictures and explains them in her home language to a friend.

Arif (6 years)

Arif can already read simple words and count to 100. He finishes quickly, gets bored with repeated tasks, and sometimes disrupts others. Waiting for a turn and sharing are hard for him.

Have a go

Start with what each child can do. Then think about what may get in the way. Try not to start with a label.

Pace and choice. Child-centred practice respects each child’s natural pace of development and learning, and gives children freedom to choose from activities that suit their needs, interests and potential. The “slow education” idea is less content, a slower pace and deeper involvement (Catalano et al., 2023).

Change the pathway, keep the goalUbah jalurnya, pertahankan tujuannya

Build understanding

Capability kemampuan

What the child can already do, know or contribute.

Barrier hambatan

What stops the child showing it: the task, the room, the language, the pace, the way of responding.

Adjustment penyesuaian

A change that removes the barrier. It should not change the learning goal.

Change the pathway when the barrier is about access. Keep the learning goal.
Have a go

What kind of change is it? Choose one for each. You will see a short explanation.

Lina may explain her thinking first to a friend in her home language, then to the class.

Dewa may stand and move between stations while the story is read.

Dewa takes part only in a colouring corner, away from the group.

Arif is invited to make the price signs and check the others' counting.

Lina is asked to repeat words after the teacher until she says them correctly.

All children may show their counting with objects, drawings or words.

Have a go

Now choose one child and design two adjustments for the experience you chose.

Where does technology help, and where does it get in the way?Di mana teknologi membantu, dan di mana menghambat?

Build understanding

Technology can be unplugged (tanpa perangkat digital), with no devices, or plugged (dengan perangkat), with robots or tablets. Unplugged examples from the research include children giving each other direction cards, putting story cards in order, and acting out the steps of “bathing the baby” or “dressing for a party”.

A review of 26 studies of computational thinking (berpikir komputasional: step-by-step problem solving such as sequencing, spotting patterns and fixing mistakes) found that children aged 3 to 8 could build these early skills, and also communication and collaboration, when activities suited their age (Su & Yang, 2023).

Low floor, high ceiling

Many tools are easy to start with (a low floor) and still let children build more complex solutions (a high ceiling), as reported in Su & Yang (2023). That could suit children who need different starting points, such as Lina and Arif.

Questions the review leaves open

Few valid, reliable ways to assess young children’s computational thinking; choosing tools and designing age-appropriate activities is hard; most studies come from developed countries.

Equity

Girls tend to be put off by boy-dominated robotics toys, which may lead to digital inequity. The review found no studies on whether socioeconomic status or gender affects children’s computational thinking.

Have a go

Try an unplugged idea. In the pretend market, children give each other direction cards to walk to a stall and buy something, step by step.

A debate for your class or staff room: What is the role of technology in a young child’s day?

Is the support helping or hiding the learning?Apakah dukungan membantu atau menutupi pembelajaran?

Build understanding

A support helps when the child does more of their own thinking over time. It hides learning when the support, or the adult, does the thinking for the child.

What you notice here becomes the evidence you will work with in the next module on assessment.
