What you will be able to doApa yang akan dapat Anda lakukan

  1. Explain what holistic development means in early childhood education and why areas of development are interconnected.
  2. Recognise opportunities for multiple aspects of children’s development within everyday play, routines, inquiry and learning experiences.
  3. Use curriculum goals or intentions to develop a clear focus for learning and a lens for observation without fragmenting the child’s experience into isolated domains.
  4. Design or refine an integrated learning experience that intentionally supports multiple aspects of children’s development.
  5. Reflect on how holistic planning can be adapted to their own children, setting, community and curriculum context.

Seeing the whole childMelihat anak secara utuh

Build understanding

We can look at a child through five lenses. They help us think about development; they are not separate curriculum subjects.

Development does not happen in boxes

Language develops through relationships. Physical experiences can involve thinking and problem solving. Play involves emotional regulation.

Every area shapes the others

Each area shapes, and is shaped by, the others.

One child. One experience.

One experience can hold many possibilities for learning.

Most actions touch several areas at once. For example, planning where things go involves language, problem solving and negotiating with others. Watering and waiting involves responsibility and care, patience, and early scientific thinking. There is no single right answer.

Build understanding

One experience, interconnected learning: communication, creativity, problem solving, responsibility and care, mathematical and scientific thinking, collaboration and self-regulation, and physical coordination.

And what is the educator doing? Choosing, listening, noticing, questioning, providing, extending, revisiting.

Child-centred does not mean educator-passive. Intentional teaching is active, thoughtful and responsive.

4about 10 min

Holistic does not mean everything at onceHolistik tidak berarti semuanya sekaligus

Build understanding

Not this

Artificially ticking every box. Attaching every developmental area to an activity so the plan looks “complete”.

But this

Planning with intention. Focusing deliberately on the learning that matters, while staying open to the connected learning that emerges.

Have a go

Which of these plans falls into the trap?

Plan A. The plan lists five activities, one for each area: literacy, mathematics, science, art and social-emotional.

Plan B. Children plan and create a small garden. The educator listens, asks questions and notes what the children try.

Plan C. A garden activity has a tick-sheet attached: “Have I covered every developmental area?”

Compare with some ideas

Plans A and C are checklist thinking: they start from areas to cover. Plan B is holistic thinking: it starts from the children and notices interconnected learning as it happens. “Have I covered every developmental area?” is not the question to ask.

5about 10 min

Direction, not a checklistArah, bukan daftar centang

Build understanding

The curriculum is a compass kompas, not a checklist. It gives direction: a curriculum intention.

Checklist thinking

  • Starts with outcomes to tick off
  • Plans activities to “cover” each area
  • Success = everything covered
  • Children fit around the plan

Holistic thinking

  • Starts with the children and a curriculum intention
  • Plans meaningful experiences
  • Notices interconnected learning as it happens
  • The plan responds to children
Have a go

Choose one goal or intention from your own curriculum and write it in your own words.

6about 25 min

From interest to intention to experienceDari minat ke tujuan ke pengalaman

Build understanding

Planning moves through three questions: Know and notice (What do I know about, or have I noticed about, the children?), Intention (What learning might I intentionally support?), and Experience (What experience or environment could enable it?).

A worked example: Children keep crowding around ants on the playground.
What might I want them to explore or develop? To investigate, notice and talk about what they see.
What could make this meaningful? It starts from their own interest.
Where are the choices? Children decide what to look at, how to record it and what to ask next.
